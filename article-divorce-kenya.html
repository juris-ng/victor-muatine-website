How Long Does a Divorce Actually Take in Kenya? A Step-by-Step Guide

6 min read April 2, 2026 Victor Muatine

One of the most common questions people ask us is: "How long will my divorce take?" The honest answer is — it depends. Under the Marriage Act, 2014 (No. 4 of 2014), Kenya recognised five categories of marriage: civil, Christian, customary, Hindu, and Islamic. The dissolution of each follows the same general High Court procedure, but the timeline varies dramatically depending on whether your divorce is contested or uncontested, and whether children or property are involved.

The Two Tracks: Contested vs Uncontested

Understanding this distinction is the single most important thing you can do before filing. An uncontested divorce is one where both spouses agree on the fact of divorce and on all ancillary issues — custody, maintenance, and division of property. An uncontested matter in the High Court (Family Division) typically concludes in six to twelve months, sometimes faster if dockets are uncrowded.

A contested divorce — where spouses disagree on any of those issues — can take anywhere from one to four years, occasionally longer. Contested matters involving property disputes, cross-examination of witnesses, or allegations of adultery significantly extend the timeline.

Legal Basis

Divorce jurisdiction sits primarily with the High Court (Family Division). Section 66 of the Marriage Act, 2014 governs grounds for dissolution. The court will also apply the Matrimonial Property Act, 2013 for property division, and the Children Act, 2022 for custody and maintenance.

Grounds for Divorce in Kenya

You cannot file for divorce simply because you are unhappy in your marriage. Section 66 of the Marriage Act, 2014 provides specific grounds. The most commonly relied-upon are:

  • Adultery — your spouse had sexual intercourse with another person outside the marriage
  • Cruelty — conduct that makes it unreasonable to expect continued cohabitation (this includes physical and psychological abuse)
  • Desertion — your spouse has deserted you for at least three years
  • Irretrievable breakdown — the broadest ground, covering situations where the marriage has simply ceased to function and cannot be saved

Step-by-Step: The Divorce Process

  1. 1
    Consult an advocate and file a Petition

    Your advocate drafts a petition setting out the marriage details, the ground relied upon, and the relief sought (dissolution, custody, maintenance, property orders). This is filed at the High Court registry together with the marriage certificate and court fees.

  2. 2
    Service on the Respondent

    The petition must be served on your spouse personally. If your spouse cannot be found, you may apply to serve by substituted service (notice in a newspaper or other means). Contested timelines often stall here — an evasive spouse can delay service by months.

  3. 3
    Respondent files an Answer

    The respondent has a prescribed period (usually 15 days from service) to file an Answer. If they do not respond, you may apply for an ex parte hearing and proceed by default.

  4. 4
    Directions and Pre-trial Conference

    The court holds a conference to determine the issues in dispute, the number of witnesses, and a hearing schedule. This is where contested matters begin to diverge significantly in timeline.

  5. 5
    Hearing and Judgment

    Witnesses give evidence, documents are produced, and the judge delivers judgment. In uncontested matters, this is often a formality. In contested matters, this is the most time-consuming stage.

  6. 6
    Decree Nisi, then Decree Absolute

    The court first grants a Decree Nisi — a provisional divorce order. After a waiting period (typically three months), you apply to make it a Decree Absolute, which is the final order dissolving the marriage.

What Slows a Divorce Down?

Four things consistently extend timelines: disputes over children (custody, access, maintenance), property disputes (especially where one spouse disputes ownership of matrimonial property), non-cooperation by one spouse, and court backlogs. The Nairobi High Court Family Division is among the busiest divisions in the country.

"Most people come to me thinking a divorce is a form you fill. It is a proceeding with real legal consequences — for property, for children, for financial security. Starting with proper advice is the single best investment you can make."

A Note on Children

If there are minor children, the court will not grant a dissolution without making orders regarding their welfare. Under the Children Act, 2022, the best interests of the child are the paramount consideration. Courts routinely order custody, access schedules, and maintenance payments before finalising dissolution. If you and your spouse agree on these arrangements, they can be recorded as a consent order — which is faster, less expensive, and less adversarial.

What You Should Do Now

If you are considering a divorce, do not move out of the matrimonial home or make financial decisions before speaking to an advocate. Either action can prejudice your position — particularly on property matters. The earlier you get proper legal advice, the more options you have.
