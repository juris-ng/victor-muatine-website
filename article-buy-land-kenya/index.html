Before You Buy Land in Kenya: The 7 Due Diligence Steps You Cannot Skip

9 min read March 15, 2026 Advocate Mugumya

Land fraud is one of the most persistent and devastating legal problems in Kenya. Every year, Kenyans lose their life savings to fraudulent land transactions — double allocations, forged title deeds, land sold without the knowledge of the registered owner, and properties with hidden encumbrances. Most of these losses are preventable. These seven due diligence steps are the foundation of any safe land transaction.

The Governing Framework

Land transactions in Kenya are governed primarily by the Land Act, 2012, the Land Registration Act, 2012, and the Physical and Land Use Planning Act, 2019. The National Land Commission Act, 2012 applies to public land. Stamp duty is payable under the Stamp Duty Act (Cap 480).

The 7 Due Diligence Steps

  1. 1
    Conduct an Official Search at the Land Registry

    The single most important step. An official search reveals who is actually registered as the owner of the parcel, whether there are any encumbrances (mortgages, charges, cautions, or restrictions), and whether the title is under any court orders. Request the search yourself — do not rely on a copy presented by the seller. A genuine title in a seller's hands can co-exist with a fraudulent one at the registry. Search directly at the relevant Land Registry (Nairobi, Mombasa, Nakuru, etc.) or through the Ardhisasa digital land platform for parcels that have been migrated to the system.

  2. 2
    Obtain a Copy of the Registry Index Map (RIM) or Survey Plan

    The Survey of Kenya maintains maps showing the exact boundaries of every registered parcel. A copy of the RIM or survey plan confirms that the physical land you are being shown corresponds to the parcel described in the title deed. Boundary disputes are common where sellers show buyers a larger piece of land than what is actually registered.

  3. 3
    Check for Outstanding Land Rates and Rent

    Outstanding land rent (payable to the national government for leasehold land) and land rates (payable to the county government for freehold and leasehold land within a municipality) are a charge on the land. If you buy without clearing these arrears, you inherit the debt. Request a Land Rent Clearance Certificate from the Kenya Revenue Authority and a Land Rates Clearance Certificate from the relevant county government.

  4. 4
    Verify the Land Use / Zoning

    Confirm with the relevant county government planning department what the land is zoned for — residential, commercial, agricultural, industrial, or mixed use. This matters because you cannot lawfully build a commercial structure on land zoned exclusively for agriculture, and vice versa. A number of buyers have discovered, after purchase, that their "residential plot" falls within a road reserve or a riparian buffer zone where no construction is permitted.

  5. 5
    Conduct a Physical Visit and Confirm Actual Possession

    Visit the land in person. Confirm that the seller is in actual possession and that no third party is occupying, farming, or claiming rights over it. Adverse possession claims (Section 37 of the Limitation of Actions Act) can arise where a person has been in open, continuous, and hostile possession for 12 years. Equally, confirm there are no squatters, no access disputes with neighbouring parcels, and that beacons marking the boundaries are in place and match the survey plan.

  6. 6
    Search for Cautions, Caveats, Court Orders, and Restrictions

    The official search (Step 1) covers registered encumbrances, but also check whether any court orders have been obtained against the land or the seller. Search the High Court cause list. A seller involved in active litigation over the property may not be able to pass a clean title — and a transfer registered in the face of a court order can be set aside. Your advocate can advise on targeted court searches.

  7. 7
    Engage an Advocate for the Conveyancing

    The conveyancing process — drafting the agreement for sale, transfer documents, applying for consent where required, paying stamp duty, and registering the transfer — is a legal process that must be handled by an Advocate of the High Court of Kenya. Attempting it without one, or relying on the seller's advocate (who has a conflict of interest), is the most common way transactions go wrong. Your advocate will also conduct the formal due diligence searches on your behalf and advise you on any risks discovered.

"In 25 years of practice, I have never seen a victim of land fraud who did the seven due diligence steps properly. Every single one of them skipped at least one. The cost of proper due diligence is a fraction of the cost of litigation — or of losing the land entirely."

A Note on Leasehold Land

If you are buying leasehold land, check the remaining term of the lease. Banks are generally reluctant to lend against a leasehold title with fewer than 30–40 years remaining. Renewal of government leases requires an application to the National Land Commission and involves payment of a new premium — a cost many buyers do not anticipate.

Spousal Consent for Matrimonial Property

Under Section 12 of the Matrimonial Property Act, 2013, a spouse cannot sell, mortgage, or transfer matrimonial property without the written consent of the other spouse. If you are buying from a married person, confirm whether the property is matrimonial property and, if it is, obtain the written consent of the seller's spouse. Failure to do so can result in the sale being set aside.
